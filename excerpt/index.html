The Three Doors

Every productive conversation moves through three phases in order. Skip one and it breaks.

If you are the one deciding

This chapter is written to the person doing the selling. Read it anyway, because every door the seller should open is one you can check for. Did the meeting feel safe enough for you to say "I don't understand"? Did they understand your problem before they proposed a solution to it? Did you walk out with a next step that someone owns, or with a good feeling? If one of those answers is no, someone skipped a door, and the offer in front of you was built on the wrong side of it.

The call started badly before anyone said anything important.

The prospect had agreed to 30 minutes. The salesperson opened with a two-minute company overview, clicked to a slide titled "Our Core Capabilities" and started talking about the platform. The prospect had their arms folded before the third slide.

The sequence had gone wrong. The product was fine and the price had not come up yet, but the salesperson had walked through the wrong door first, and once that happens the conversation never quite recovers.

Every productive conversation moves through three phases in the same order, whether it is 15 minutes with a new contact or a three-hour workshop with a client you have worked with for two years: Safety, then Context, then Decision. Skip one and something breaks. The client stays guarded, the conversation stays shallow, and you talk about the right things for the wrong reasons and walk out wondering what happened.

I call this The Three Doors because the image does the work: three doors, in order, and the next one opens only once you have walked through the first.

Door one: Safety

Safety is the state in which the other person is willing to tell you the truth. Small talk fills silence; safety makes the room a place where the client says "I don't know", tells you the real problem instead of the official version of it, and pushes back on your thinking instead of nodding and then deciding privately to walk away. A client who can object openly is worth more to you than one who smiles and says nothing.

You build it by humanising the conversation before the agenda starts. You ask about something real, you show up as a person with a name and a morning, and you let the fact that you belong in the meeting prove itself. The emotional temperature of a conversation is set in the first two minutes, and after that you are managing it.

Door two: Context

Context is shared understanding built before any solution is proposed. The common mistake runs the other way: the salesperson arrives with a list of qualification questions and works through it, taking notes, assembling a picture of the client's world that serves the next proposal. The client can feel the form being filled in.

"What are you trying to achieve?" starts a conversation. "What features do you need?" starts a form. When the Context door opens properly, the client is explaining the problem in their own words and the two of you are building the picture together, in the meeting, so that the alignment exists before anyone writes anything down.

This is why proposals that skip real discovery land with a thud. The client reads it and thinks: this is close to my problem, and close is not enough to sign.

Door three: Decision

Decision is naming something specific: an outcome, an owner, a next step and a date. Closing is what people do when they have missed the first two doors and are forcing the conversation to an outcome it was never ready for.

"We should get together again sometime" was said on that call. "I'll send a summary of what we discussed and you'll share it with your operations lead before Friday" is a decision, because someone owns something when they leave the room.

A conversation that ends without a next step has stalled because nobody named the thing that needed to happen next. Naming it is your job.

The meeting never felt safe enough for a real conversation. The context never got built because the pitch started before the understanding did. The decision never got named because everyone was too polite to make it concrete.

The Three Doors guarantee that you had the right conversation, and that is all any method can guarantee. If the right conversation does not end in a yes, the fit was wrong, and you have learned that at the cost of 30 minutes.
